Who Owns the Digital Public Square? Social Media and the First Amendment

Gabriella Ruffels

I. The Digital Public Square

Social media serves as a prominent venue for public discourse in the modern age. Through the sharing of images, videos, and bodies of text, public officials are connected to their constituents and held directly accountable for their choices while in office. These platforms – Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and the like – facilitate public debate, yet they are controlled by private companies. This raises a significant constitutional question: how does the First Amendment govern expression when private ownership and public engagement overlap?

The First Amendment prohibits the government from “abridging the freedom of speech or of the press.”1 The application of the First Amendment to social media should depend on who exercises control over speech and in what capacity he or she does so. Moody v. NetChoice acknowledges that social media platforms may have a constitutional right to curate expression in its entirety,2 while Lindke v. Freed establishes when public officials’ social media activity becomes subject to legal constraints.3 A joint interpretation of the cases reveals a necessity for nuance when applying traditional public-private distinctions to digital spaces that serve as both public and private forums.

II. Moody v. NetChoice

In 2021, Florida and Texas passed laws regulating large social media platforms’ ability to curate content.4 Both states were concerned about platforms perpetuating political bias by unfairly suppressing certain speakers or viewpoints. Florida’s law restricted platforms from removing or deprioritizing certain content, including content from political candidates and news outlets.5 Texas’s law more broadly restricted censoring users based on their viewpoints.6 Both laws required explanations from the private owners for content moderation.

NetChoice, a trade association representing the technology companies, argued that the laws interfered with the platforms’ First Amendment right to freedom of expression.7 The Eleventh Circuit ruled against Florida’s law,8 while the Fifth Circuit upheld the Texas law.9 In 2024, the Supreme Court vacated both decisions and remanded the cases for further proceedings, underscoring that content curation qualifies as a protected expressive activity under the First Amendment.10

III. Lindke v. Freed

James Freed’s Facebook page showcased a variety of posts: some personal and others reflecting his work as city manager of Port Huron, Michigan.11 Kevin Lindke expressed his dissatisfaction with the city’s response to COVID-19 in Freed’s comment section.12 Freed deleted Lindke’s comments and eventually blocked him.13 The First Amendment restricts government censorship, but it does not govern private decisions about whom to allow access to a personal account.14 The constitutional issue in this case is whether Freed acted as a government official or private individual.

In response, the Supreme Court developed a two-part test.15 An official’s social media activity constitutes government action when the official both (1) has authority to speak on the government’s behalf about the relevant matter and (2) appears to exercise that authority in the relevant posts.16 This test ensures that a public official cannot avoid constitutional scrutiny by calling an account personal and also protects his or her right to practice privacy on social media.

IV. Private Rights and Public Accountability

Moody asserts that the government cannot wholly restrict private platforms’ editorial and algorithmic choices.17 Lindke sets a precedent for evaluating a public official’s conduct on social media and whether or not it is attributable to the government.18 Both cases require courts to determine who controls online speech and whether that speech is protected under the First Amendment or restricted by constitutional limits. The fact that social media serves as a public forum should not automatically eliminate private platforms’ right to free expression. Conversely, a public official should not be able to subvert constitutional responsibilities by using a personal account to exercise government authority.

Consider a mayor who uses one Instagram page to publish family photographs, personal opinions, and official emergency announcements. If the mayor blocks a critic, the Lindke test can be applied to determine whether the act is constitutional. By examining the mayor’s ability to speak on behalf of the local government and whether she seems to do so in her interaction with the critic (notwithstanding public posts), the court can rule on the incident through a public-governmental or private-personal lens accordingly.

Public-private distinctions can be difficult to impose when personal and official communications coexist on the same account; however, treating every online space used for public discourse as a government-controlled forum violates private editorial freedom. It follows, therefore, that the application of the First Amendment should focus on the identity of the actor, the authority being exercised, and the nature of the action, rather than whether the digital space serves a significant public purpose.

V. Conclusion

When considering the question of who owns the digital public square, it is crucial to distinguish between personal and governmental actors. While states cannot restrict private companies’ content curation, the First Amendment can protect digital space as a public forum when officials exercise their authority online.19 Moody and Lindke offer complementary, rather than contradictory, precedents that introduce room for nuance in social media cases regarding First Amendment rights. Constitutional doctrine must discern private control over expression from the exercise of governmental power without generalizing online spaces as inherently public or private.
