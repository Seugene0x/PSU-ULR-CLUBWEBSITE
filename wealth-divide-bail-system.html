Exploring the Wealth Divide in America’s Bail System

Gabriella Ruffels

I. Introduction

The American criminal justice system allows individuals accused of crimes to walk free in the time leading up to their trials as long as they are able to pay a certain amount of money.1 Individuals who cannot afford this bail may remain incarcerated, even though a court has not yet convicted them and they are presumed innocent.2 In this way, the cash bail system raises constitutional concerns surrounding equal protection because it allows economic inequality to influence pretrial freedom.3

II. The American Cash Bail System

The term “bail” refers to financial security required from someone accused of a crime in exchange for their release while awaiting trial.4 Because cash bail is refunded after the individual attends all necessary legal proceedings, it encourages defendants to return for court appearances.5 Moreover, it allows them to walk free while their cases are pending. Bail for an arrested individual is set by a judge who determines whether the individual can be released.6 If the individual cannot afford that amount, they may remain in jail until their case is resolved. This creates an economic divide: wealthier defendants can afford bail and return home while poorer defendants may remain incarcerated. Both individuals are presumed innocent until proven guilty, regardless of their financial circumstances, but they experience vastly different versions of the justice system.

III. Equal Protection Concerns

The Fourteenth Amendment requires states to provide equal protection of the laws to individuals.7 The government cannot impose unjustified unequal treatment on individuals, but the cash bail system calls this principle into question. Consider two people facing similar charges who receive the same bail amount. A wealthy individual can afford to pay and remain free, but an impoverished individual cannot afford to pay and remains incarcerated. Although the bail amount is identical, the consequences are different because of varying economic circumstances. Therefore, the cash bail system creates different standards of justice based on wealth.

IV. Pugh v. Rainwater (1978)

Indigent defendants in Florida challenged typical monetary bail practices in a 1971 class action lawsuit.8 While the case was pending, Florida expanded its judicial system to include non-monetary alternatives for defendants experiencing poverty. In 1978, the Fifth Circuit addressed the constitutionality of relying on cash bail without adequately considering nonfinancial release options.9 It upheld that imprisoning someone solely because of poverty is unconstitutional; however, the state has a legitimate interest in ensuring defendants appear for trial.10 The case reinforced the previous standard that bail conditions must be reasonably related to guaranteeing the defendant is present in court—meaning monetary bail remains constitutional.11

V. Bearden v. Georgia (1983)

In 1983, a man was sentenced to probation and ordered to pay fines and restitution, but he could not afford the payments due to the fact that he was unemployed and lacking in financial resources.12 As a result, his probation was revoked.13 The Supreme Court ruled that individuals cannot automatically be imprisoned for failing to pay court-ordered fines when they genuinely lack the financial capacity to do so.14 It held that courts must consider the individual’s financial circumstances and alternatives to incarceration.15

Although this case involves fines and probation rather than bail, it demonstrates the same constitutional concerns that indigent defendants and monetary bail raise. It sets a precedent for the courts to consider equal protection in the face of financial inequality in the justice system, and it supports the argument that poverty alone should not determine whether someone loses their freedom.16

VI. Alternatives & Counterarguments

A possible alternative to cash bail is release on recognizance, or the idea that defendants legally promise to return to court.17 Instead of providing money, the defendant signs an agreement to appear at all scheduled court hearings.18 Additional legal consequences take effect if the defendant fails to return to court.19 Other alternatives to monetary bail include regular check-ins with a pretrial services officer, restrictions on travel, and electronic monitoring of the defendant.20 Each of these ensures that the defendants appear in court for trial without requiring them to pay money in exchange for their freedom.

Still, public safety concerns may justify pretrial incarceration under appropriate circumstances. It is critical that courts consider each defendant’s individual circumstances to judge whether a defendant can be safely released without collecting bail. Factors like previous failures to appear in court, the seriousness of the alleged offense, and relevant criminal history can help courts make this decision.

VII. Conclusion

In summation, the American cash bail system raises concerns about the relationship between wealth and equal treatment under the law.21 As demonstrated in the above cases, an individual’s financial circumstances should not dictate their freedom.22 While ensuring court appearances and protecting public safety are legitimate government interests, alternatives to monetary bail offer ways that courts can pursue these goals without making freedom dependent on wealth.23 The ability to afford bail should not determine who gets to remain free before being proven guilty.
