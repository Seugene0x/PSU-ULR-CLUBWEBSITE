Restoring Integrity & Transparency: The Protect College Sports Act

Joshua Crawley

I. The Introduction of the NIL Post-Alston Case

In June of 2021, the court case NCAA vs. Alston ruled that athletes receiving education-related compensation violated antitrust laws.1 In July of 2021, the NCAA allowed college athletes to begin profiting from their name, image, and likeness due to external pressures that followed the Alston case. Now, in 2026, NIL deals have exploded into an unregulated industry. There are two real issues, one being that athletes aren’t educated in terms of corporate branding and licensing agreements, which makes them vulnerable to exploitation.2 NIL refers to the ability of athletes to receive money from third parties for the use of their name, image, and likeness, typically to endorse a product.3 For example, Caitlin Clark would endorse Gatorade on social media and show up in Nike commercials, wherein she would be compensated. The second issue is NIL operations have lacked a framework or any public reporting standards; third parties and donors have been able to operate under the table. This fundamental lack of transparency has made it impossible to distinguish genuine endorsement deals from covert violation incidents. With no clear visibility about where funds were going or how players were being compensated, universities began competing in a borderline ludicrous environment. The situation is not a crisis. Rather, it’s the result of decades of incompetence by the NCAA and their inability to lay down a framework that regulates how teams could spend and how players could earn.4

II. The Catalyst for Reform

The case Johnson vs. NCAA in 2019 contended that student-athletes who engaged in interscholastic activities for their respective universities were employees and should be compensated for the time they spent on those activities. That set the precedent for the future clarification of the law in 2024.5 The 2024 decision in that case ruled that student-athletes could legally be considered employees based on the economic reality of the situation, which opened the can of worms for NIL to explode.6 While the ruling was made, no clear rules about direct compensation or third-party deals were put in place. The ruling led to schools across the country pouring millions into athlete recruitment and retention to stay competitive because college athletics had turned into an NFL free agency system.

Athletes making money was never the issue, but rather the fact that these court rulings never clearly defined how athletes could profit, leaving it up to interpretation of the schools and athletes themselves. It was impossible to regulate under the table deals, where players were potentially being paid for something other than playing their sport. On top of that, agent exploitation is happening everywhere. NIL has been heavily unregulated and has allowed athletes to profit without actually doing anything. Schools then pour millions of dollars into programs, which leaves most schools in heavy debt and hurts the integrity of the sport. 94% of D1 programs spend more money than they generate, and that is a problem.7 It puts schools in precarious positions financially and in an endless cycle of debt. For the athletes, schools easily take advantage of them, and they don’t profit properly in the way the law has set out for them, either. A framework is necessary to protect the athletes, collegiate athletics, and everyone involved.

III. The Protect College Sports Act and Core Provisions

The Protect College Sports Act aims to protect the name, image, and likeness rights of student athletes and provide protections for them while promoting fair competition among intercollegiate athletics.8 In short, it aims to provide an even playing field for all competing institutions nationwide while still allowing athletes to profit off of their hard work.9 Student athletes will now be required to report covered NIL agreements to their schools and the applicable intercollegiate athletic association.10 Schools will be required to report the numbers, average value, and total value of all compensation provided or promised to student athletes.11 The goal of all these measures is to create new levels of transparency in terms of where money is going and how it is being distributed.

The new eligibility rules will establish a max of five years of playing time beginning at enrollment and a 24-year age cap.12 Section 118 of the Protect College Sports Act gives the NCAA legal authority to enforce these rules and crack down on recruiting inducements.13 Another important provision is section 102, which covers agents. It updates the federal athlete-agent law to cover NIL activity, require agent registration, prohibit deceptive conduct, and cap agent fees at 5%.14 This gives students protection and the private right of action against abusive or fraudulent agents. Section 105 covers academic protections, which was something many people outside of the institutions raised as a highly important concern. It protects student athletes from Athletic Department pressure over course or major selection while allowing intercollegiate athletic associations and schools to limit time on the field to ensure progress towards a degree.15 It also protects athletes in the case of an injury by ensuring that scholarship money cannot be reduced or revoked due to performance, injuries, or roster management decisions. In short, the Protect College Sports Act provides athletes with many more benefits than just a paycheck.

IV. Protecting and Educating Athletes

The Protect College Sports Act is a vital step forward for the future of the new era of college athletics. The laws in place will go a long way towards safeguarding athletes while simultaneously reining in the unchecked spending across the country that has programs buried in debt. Federal oversight, though, needs to be paired with athlete education to truly be effective. Many athletes receive more money than they’ve ever seen before in college and don’t know how to manage it. While they should have generational wealth, many end up going bankrupt due to not being irresponsible and uneducated on how to make it last. A necessary provision that needs to come on top of the Protect College Sports Act is education for the student athletes that would also serve as protection. Collegiate athletic departments are starting to hire staff directly to handle NIL and serve as entrepreneurial advisors to their athletes.16 According to an NCAA survey from 2022 when NIL was starting, collegiate athletes reported needing the most education in tax and financial literacy (49%), career planning (47%), navigating NIL (40%), and professional opportunities (37%).17 These resources have helped athletes deal with the complicated world of NIL and how money flows and works. This legislation protects athletes during their college years but also sets them up for success in whatever they step into afterwards, whether going on to play professionally or down a different path.

V. Conclusion

The Protect College Sports Act will go into effect after House approval, and most conferences are happy with that. Every team in the Power Four Conferences (Big Ten, SEC, Big 12, ACC) supports the bill.18 This legislation is significant in that some of the biggest and most powerful athletic departments and programs in the country want to see this bill pushed through. The law has been framed as the “last best hope” to establish a federal governance framework for collegiate athletics following the industry's rapid recent transformation.19 College sports have gone haywire since the introduction of NIL because nobody bothered to put anything in place to monitor or control it. The law will restore control of the athletes to those who have exploited them for their own gain. It is vital that the bill pass for the future of college athletics and the well-being of everyone involved. The Protect College Sports Act represents a long-overdue compromise. College athletes will still be able to profit and capitalize on their personal brand. Protect College Sports Act regulations, though, will safeguard the athletes and their futures as well as the long-term health of college athletics. With universal standards across the board, college athletics will avoid collapsing into an unstable financial system and instead secure a sustainable future for programs and student-athletes to thrive.
